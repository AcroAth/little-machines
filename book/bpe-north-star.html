The old way was a dictionary. A word-level tokenizer keeps a long list of known words and swaps each one for its number. Fast, tidy — until the world hands it a word that isn't on the list.

When it meets a word it has never seen — say, snorfle — it ····· .

Every unknown word collapses into the same gray stand-in — UNK, short for unknown. The model can't tell snorfle from qwyx — both are just UNK, and the meaning is gone before it starts.

So drop the dictionary. Go the other way — smaller, past words, past letters, all the way down to bytes, the raw numbers a computer already stores everything in.

There are only possible byte values2, and every text ever written — every language, every emoji, every typo — is just some sequence of them.

Now nothing is ever unknown again. But look what it cost: the sentence is in crumbs, one tiny piece per byte. We traded UNK for length.

Here's the fix for the length — and it's the whole idea. Start from the crumbs, then let the text itself tell you which pieces to glue back together. The rule is simple: find the pair that shows up most, and fuse it into one. (Bytes already claim the numbers 0–255, so every new token takes the next free id — your very first will be 256.)

You did four merges. GPT-2 did fifty thousand — the very same loop, run on a big slice of the internet. The trick is old: it began life as a way to compress files, got borrowed for translation, and GPT-2 — an early ancestor of today's chatbots — was the first big model to read this way.1

BPE · your merges

So here's where we landed. Characters alone are too small, whole words are too brittle, and a fixed dictionary always meets something it can't name.

BPE splits the difference. It lets the data vote on where the seams go: common stuff gets short, rare stuff stays splittable, and nothing is ever unknown.

It's a heuristic, not a law — just an effective one. Which is why nearly every model still reaches for it first.5
