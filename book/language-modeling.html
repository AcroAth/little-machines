The guessing game

~12 min Reveal all steps

Read this out loud and stop where it stops. It's a real sentence from a Stanford lecture — the one this whole chapter is drawn from.

pick what comes next

You just did the only thing a language model knows how to do. Not spelling, not grammar, not meaning — just this: given some words, guess the next one.

That's the whole definition. A language model assigns a probability to what comes next.1 Everything else in this book — every trick, every billion dollars of hardware — is about playing this one game better.

the page continues when you play...

But you cheated a little. You picked one word and threw all your weight behind it. A model is more honest than that: it doesn't pick — it spreads its belief across every option, before the answer arrives.

Here's the same kind of round. This time you have ten drops of belief. Pour them however you like — all on one word if you're sure, or spread thin if you're not.

drag your belief onto the words — or tap a word to drop one

That's all "probability" ever means here: how much belief you'd pour on each word before you see the answer. A good guesser is simply one that's less surprised, more often.5

Now meet the thing that does this for a living. It's small, and it has read exactly one book: this lecture, and nothing else in the world.6 Watch it play the rounds you just played.

No cleverness in there — just counting. It has no idea what any word means. When it beats you, it's because the lecture repeated a pattern; when you beat it, it's because you know things it never read.1

Here's the twist that changes everything. Anything that can score the next word can also choose one — pick a word from its belief, write it down, then guess again from there. Scoring and writing are the same coin, flipped.

roll again

Locally it is language — the level start is the lecture word for word, and even the scraps after the step are real. But watch the line itself wander off the cliff: a few words in it steps off the only sentence it was actually following, and from there it stumbles downhill, scrap by scrap, going nowhere. That babble — fluent but aimless — is exactly what machines like this one supplied to translation and speech systems for decades: not the understanding, just the part that kept the output sounding like a person.1

Why does it wander? Because it barely remembers what it just said. Right now, to choose each word, it looks back at just 2 of the words it already wrote. Drag that number down and watch the same roll fall apart.

No memory at all is noise. One word is a drunk kind of fluency. Two, and it writes in whole phrases lifted straight from the lecture — one book isn't much of a world to remember.

Hold onto that lever. Every chapter of this book's history is, underneath, one more answer to the same question: how much should the guesser remember, and what should it do with what it remembers?

That question has a seventy-year answer. The same game, played by better and better guessers. Walk the little machine along it.1 2

drag it

Every one of those was, at heart, a guesser assigning probability to what comes next — Shannon's counts and today's giants included. What changed was memory, and how the remembering is done.

Even the phrase "a language model" keeps moving. In a single decade it went from something you fine-tune, to something you prompt, to something you chat with, to something you hand a long task to

fine-tune→ prompt→ chat with→ hand a long task to

— the same engine, asked for far more each time: much longer memory, and an efficiency that matters more every year. Yet underneath, the fundamentals barely moved — the chips and the small programs ("kernels") that run on them, the gradient-style training, the transformer and its attention. Guessing what comes next is still the default way these models are taught.3 5

So why build one from scratch, when you could just prompt a finished one? Because every abstraction leaks. Prompt a model and you're standing on top of a tower you can't see into; the day it can't do what you need, there's no recourse — no lever to pull. Real understanding comes from building the thing.4

One question before you go. Across all four of those eras, the thing that never changed is ····· .

your turn — interrogate it.

Type anything. The machine shows you its live belief about what comes next — its real counts from the lecture, as blue weight on each word. Give it something the lecture never said, and watch it shrug and fall back on a shorter memory.

And that's the whole of it. The game is the definition: assign probability to whatever comes next. Better guessers are the history: seventy years of remembering more, and remembering it better. The rest of this book builds one for real.

But the machine can't read a word yet — it can only count words someone already split apart for it. Before it can guess, its text has to become numbers it can hold. That's the next story: how a computer learns to read.
