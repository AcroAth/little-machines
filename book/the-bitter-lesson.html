Bigger or cleverer?

~7 min Reveal all steps

In a handful of years, machines that read and write went from lab curiosities to—if you believe the noise—drafting a fair slice of the world's computer code. There's a rumor going around about why, and it's a tidy one: only one thing really changed, it says. The machines got bigger. The clever ideas, the rumor shrugs, quietly stopped mattering.

Before we take it apart, place your bet. Here are ten drops of belief—pour them across the two explanations however you like. All on one, if you're sure; split them, if you're torn.

There's a name for this argument. People in the field call it the bitter lesson—the very fight you just bet on, over whether size or cleverness is what really carries these machines forward—and, as you'll see, it's almost always misread.1

So: size, cleverness, or the two of them tangled together? Let's stop arguing and build the thing. Here is a reading-and-writing machine, shrunk down to a single shape. How capable it is—how often it guesses right, the game you played last page—comes from just two things, and you can grab them both.

You reached it from both sides: double what you pour in, or double what you squeeze out—either one doubles the machine. The lecture this chapter follows writes that same picture as a tiny equation.

accuracy≈efficiency×resources

its accuracy is our area · its efficiency is our height · its resources are our width

The lecture's equation: accuracy is approximately efficiency times resources. Its accuracy is our area, its efficiency is our height, its resources are our width.

Accuracy is our area—how good the machine is. Efficiency is our height: what you get out for every drop you put in. Resources are our width: the input itself.1 From here on, those are our two words—efficiency for the tall edge, resources for the wide one.

Those two edges look equal. They aren't—once the machine gets big. Slide its world from your laptop out to the frontier—the largest machines anyone is building—and keep your eye on the width.

On your laptop, a 5% better method is a rounding error—you nudged it and the strip was a whisker. Out at the frontier, that very same 5% is that thin band running the full width of the machine—and its area alone is worth everything your laptop started with.

That's the asymmetry the rumor half-noticed. Down here, a method that runs twice as slow just costs you a longer wait.1 Up there, the same slowness can burn hundreds of millions of dollars—so even a five-percent gain is a fortune.2

Right. The lesson was never size over cleverness. It's that the cleverness worth having is the kind that keeps paying as the machine grows.1 Cleverness doesn't lose to size—it multiplies it. So the bigger the pour, the more every point of cleverness is worth.

One more pour. The rumor gave you two answers. The rectangle has just put a third on the table—size × cleverness, multiplied. Your first bet is still here, faded underneath the original two—pour ten again, now that you've held the shape in your own hands.

No score here, and no right answer to hand back. The page made its case; where the drops land is yours.

If cleverness is a multiplier, you go hunting for it everywhere. That is why a book like this one—and the course it reads from—treats efficiency as something close to an obsession: you measure where every second and every machine-hour actually goes, then shave away the waste. The course has a plain name for the habit—profiling and benchmarking, which is really just measuring, relentlessly, and refusing to look away from what's slow.3

The rectangle is still here—both edges unlocked now, and pulled wider than before. Push them around. Put a big number on one edge and a small one on the other, then swap. The multiplier is the whole story of this chapter, and it pays to feel it in your hands.
